## 1. Project Overview

This project documents my independent design, construction, modification, and flight testing of a radio-controlled (RC) airplane.

# 3. Aerodynamics Research

## 3.1 The Four Forces of Flight

Aircraft flight is governed by four primary forces:

| Force | Direction | Engineering Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Lift | Perpendicular to the flight path | Supports aircraft weight |
| Weight | Downward | Depends on aircraft mass and gravity |
| Thrust | Generally forward | Produced by the propulsion system |
| Drag | Opposes flight direction | Resists motion through the air |

For straight, level, unaccelerated flight:

**Lift = Weight**

**Thrust = Drag**

These conditions are useful reference points, although actual aircraft motion involves changing forces and moments.

**NASA research and illustration:**

[Four Forces on an Airplane — NASA Glenn Research Center](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/four-forces-on-an-airplane/)

## 3.2 Lift Equation

The aerodynamic lift equation is:

\[
L=\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2SC_L
\]

Where:

| Symbol | Meaning | SI Unit |
|---|---|---|
| L | Lift force | N |
| ρ | Air density | kg/m³ |
| V | Airspeed | m/s |
| S | Wing reference area | m² |
| C_L | Lift coefficient | Dimensionless |

Lift depends on airspeed, air density, wing area, and aerodynamic characteristics.

### Worked Example — Illustrative Values

The following calculation is a research example, **not a measurement of the original airplane**.

Assume:

- Air density = 1.225 kg/m³
- Airspeed = 10 m/s
- Wing area = 0.20 m²
- Lift coefficient = 0.60

Then:

\[
L=0.5(1.225)(10)^2(0.20)(0.60)
\]

\[
\boxed{L=7.35\text{ N}}
\]

The equivalent supported mass under ideal steady-level-flight assumptions is:

\[
m=\frac{7.35}{9.81}=0.749\text{ kg}
\]

This illustrates the relationship between aerodynamic lift and aircraft weight. It does not establish the actual aircraft's lifting capability.

## 3.3 Wing Geometry

Important wing measurements include wingspan, chord, wing area, and aspect ratio.

For a rectangular wing:

\[
S=bc
\]

\[
AR=\frac{b^2}{S}=\frac{b}{c}
\]

Where:

- b = wingspan
- c = wing chord
- S = wing area
- AR = aspect ratio

### Example Geometry Calculation

Assume:

- Wingspan = 1.00 m
- Chord = 0.18 m

Then:

\[
S=(1.00)(0.18)=0.18\text{ m}^2
\]

\[
AR=\frac{1.00^2}{0.18}=5.56
\]

These are illustrative dimensions only.

**Reference:** [NASA Wing Geometry](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/wing-geometry/)

# 4. Fuselage Design and Construction

## 4.1 Design Considerations

The fuselage is the main body of the aircraft and provides the structure needed to connect and support other components.

During development, I considered:

- Structural weight and material selection
- Available internal space for electrical components
- Wing and tail attachment
- Battery accessibility
- Balance and center of gravity
- Structural durability
- Aerodynamic drag

A foam-based construction approach allowed the structure to be cut, shaped, and modified during prototyping.

## 4.2 Construction Process

My development records document several important stages.

**Initial construction:** I began fabricating the fuselage and wing components, researching dimensions and working with foam materials.

**Reconstruction:** After damaging a significant portion of the structure, I rebuilt sections of the airplane and evaluated the design for improved balance and durability.

**Weight reduction:** I reconsidered the battery configuration and investigated lighter alternatives.

**Tail redesign:** I rebuilt sections of the tail and adjusted the rudder design to improve the intended control response.

**Structural reinforcement:** I worked on reinforcing the airframe and evaluating areas that required additional support.

## 6.2 Center of Gravity

The center of gravity is determined by the mass and location of individual components.

For a one-dimensional model:

\[
x_{CG}=\frac{\sum m_ix_i}{\sum m_i}
\]

### Worked Example

| Component | Mass (g) | Position from Datum (cm) | Mass × Position |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Airframe | 220 | 30 | 6,600 |
| Motor | 50 | 5 | 250 |
| ESC | 25 | 20 | 500 |
| Servos | 20 | 45 | 900 |
| Receiver | 10 | 25 | 250 |
| Battery | 60 | 20 | 1,200 |
| Wiring/adhesive | 15 | 30 | 450 |
| **Total** | **400** | — | **10,150** |

\[
x_{CG}=\frac{10150}{400}=25.375\text{ cm}
\]

**Example result:** The calculated center of gravity is approximately 25.4 cm from the chosen datum.

This is a mathematical example, not the measured balance point of the constructed aircraft.

# 7. Electrical System Research and Integration

## 7.1 System Architecture

A typical RC airplane electrical system contains:

1. Lithium-polymer battery
2. Electronic speed controller (ESC)
3. Brushless propulsion motor
4. Radio receiver
5. Servo motors
6. Handheld radio transmitter
7. Optional LED indicator and current-limiting resistor

The actual models installed in my airplane have not been fully identified.

### Conceptual Wiring Diagram

```text
     HANDHELD RADIO TRANSMITTER
                 |
             RF SIGNAL
                 |
                 v
          +--------------+
          | RC RECEIVER  |
          +--------------+
             |   |    |
       PWM---+   |    +---PWM
             |   |         |
             v   |         v
         SERVO 1 |      SERVO 2
                 |
              ESC SIGNAL
                 |
                 v
LiPo BATTERY --> ESC --> BRUSHLESS MOTOR
                 |
             BEC / 5V*
                 |
          RECEIVER & SERVOS

*Only when the ESC provides a compatible BEC.
```

**Important:** The transmitter communicates wirelessly with the receiver. It is not electrically connected to the aircraft battery.

A typical ESC connects the battery to the propulsion motor and may supply regulated power through an integrated battery eliminator circuit (BEC). Actual wiring depends on the installed hardware.

## 7.2 Electrical Component Functions

| Component | Primary Function | Connection |
|---|---|---|
| LiPo battery | Stores electrical energy | ESC power input |
| ESC | Controls propulsion motor | Battery, motor, receiver |
| Brushless motor | Produces mechanical rotation | ESC motor outputs |
| Receiver | Decodes radio commands | Transmitter RF link, servo/ESC signals |
| Servo motor | Moves a control surface | Receiver signal and regulated power |
| Transmitter | Sends pilot commands | Wireless RF link |
| LED | Provides visual indication | Suitable regulated supply and resistor |
| Resistor | Limits LED current | Series connection with LED |

## 7.3 Servo Control

Many hobby RC servos use a pulse-width control signal.

Typical reference values:

| Pulse Width | Illustrative Command |
|---|---|
| 1.0 ms | One end of travel |
| 1.5 ms | Approximately neutral |
| 2.0 ms | Opposite end of travel |
| 20 ms period | Approximately 50 Hz update rate |

Actual servo endpoints and direction depend on the model and calibration.

**Reference:** [Pololu — Servo Electrical Characteristics](https://www.pololu.com/blog/16/electrical-characteristics-of-servos-and-introduction-to-the-servo-control-interface)

# 8. LTspice Circuit Model

To support the electronics research, a conceptual LTspice model can be used to study power delivery, resistive loads, LED operation, and simulated servo-control signals.

The model is **not a complete simulation of the actual RC receiver, brushless motor, ESC, or servo mechanics**.

## 8.1 Illustrative Circuit Parameters

| Element | Example Value | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| V1 | 7.4 V DC | Nominal two-cell LiPo representation |
| VREG | 5 V DC | Idealized regulated supply |
| R1 | 330 Ω | LED current limiting |
| R2 | 1 kΩ | Illustrative resistive load |
| D1 | LED model | Visual status indicator |
| VPWM1 | 1.5 ms pulse / 20 ms period | Servo signal example |
| VPWM2 | 1.0 ms pulse / 20 ms period | Second signal example |

### LED Resistor Calculation

Assuming:

- Supply voltage = 5 V
- LED forward voltage = 2 V
- Desired current ≈ 9 mA

\[
R=\frac{V_S-V_F}{I}
\]

\[
R=\frac{5-2}{0.009}=333.3\Omega
\]

A standard 330 Ω resistor gives approximately:

\[
I=\frac{3}{330}=0.00909A
\]

\[
\boxed{I\approx9.09\text{ mA}}
\]

Estimated resistor dissipation:

\[
P=I^2R\approx0.0273W
\]

A suitably rated resistor, such as 0.25 W, provides ample margin for this illustrative circuit.

### Simplified LTspice Netlist

```spice
* RC Airplane Electronics - Educational Model
* NOT a verified physical aircraft schematic

Vbat BAT 0 DC 7.4
Vreg V5 0 DC 5

R1 V5 LED_A 330
D1 LED_A 0 DLED
.model DLED D(Is=1e-18 N=2)

R2 V5 LOAD 1k
R3 LOAD 0 1k

Vservo1 PWM1 0 PULSE(0 5 0 1u 1u 1.5m 20m)
Vservo2 PWM2 0 PULSE(0 5 0 1u 1u 1m 20m)

.tran 0 100m
.end
```

The model uses independent idealized sources. It does not model regulator efficiency, LiPo discharge behavior, servo motor current, or RF transmission.

## 8.2 Electrical Safety Considerations

- Verify actual battery voltage and polarity.
- Confirm ESC and motor compatibility.
- Verify receiver and servo voltage ratings.
- Never connect a two-cell LiPo directly to a servo rated only for lower voltage.
- Confirm whether the ESC includes a BEC.
- Avoid connecting multiple BEC outputs together without an appropriate power design.
- Remove the propeller during electrical bench testing.
- Use proper LiPo charging, storage, and handling procedures.

## 10.3 Post-Flight Analysis

The crash occurred during my first experience operating an RC aircraft.

Potential factors to evaluate include:

| Investigation Area | Why It Matters | Recommended Follow-Up |
|---|---|---|
| Pilot input | Excessive control commands may cause instability | Practice using an RC flight simulator |
| Center of gravity | Incorrect balance affects stability | Measure and verify actual CG |
| Control direction | Reversed controls may affect maneuvering | Perform preflight direction checks |
| Control throws | Excessive movement can produce aggressive responses | Adjust endpoints and rates |
| Airframe integrity | Weak joints can fail under load | Inspect and reinforce damaged areas |
| Weather | Wind and turbulence affect lightweight aircraft | Record conditions before testing |
| Electrical power | Voltage drop or connection failure can interrupt control | Inspect connectors and test under load |

These are investigation possibilities, **not confirmed explanations for the crash**.
